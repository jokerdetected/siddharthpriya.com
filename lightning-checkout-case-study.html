Paytm Payment Gateway case study

Lightning Checkout

A checkout that knows you by your phone number.

Platform
Mobile web, opened from Place order on a merchant site
Scope
Every screen and state, sign-in to payment
Status
Designed and built. First merchant integration pending

In short

One number, one code, and you are paying.

The problem

Shoppers retype the same details on every site. 17% of people who abandon a cart blame a long or complicated checkout.1

The solution

Recognise the shopper by mobile number, verify with a one-time code, then reuse their saved address and preferred way to pay.

The problem

Every store makes shoppers start from zero.

Shoppers re-enter contact, address and payment details on every merchant site. It slows them down, invites mistakes and gives them more places to quit. Merchants have no ready-made checkout that remembers a shopper they have never met.

  • let a returning shopper pay without retyping what we already know?
  • keep a first-time shopper’s address short and hard to get wrong?
  • keep price, offers and trust in view until the last step?
  • make every failure end with a next step?

The brief

Fast for returning shoppers, clear for new ones, recoverable when it fails.

Who it serves

Shoppers on a phone, returning and first-time, and merchants who add the checkout to their store.

What it must handle

Someone else’s brand on top. UPI, cards, net banking, EMI and cash on delivery. OTP, PIN code and UPI outages.

How success is measured

Checkout completion, payment success, time to pay, OTP success and address-related delivery failures.

What I took from it

Accounts cost sales

18% left when asked to create an account. Here, a mobile number and a code replace it.

Fields cost sales

Returning shoppers type two things. First-timers fill seven address fields, under the benchmark of eight.

Surprises cost sales

Extra costs are the top reason to leave, so delivery date and charge show early and the total never hides.

Failures need an exit

Every failure names the problem and offers another way to pay.

India pays its own way

UPI apps come first. Cash on delivery stays, with a visible fee.

Trust is borrowed

The checkout opens under a merchant’s brand, so verification and security cues stay on every screen.

Design principles

  • Know the shopper, don’t interrogate

    Ask only for what we cannot know.

  • Keep the price in view

    Total, offers and delivery cost never hide.

  • One decision per screen

    Side tasks open in sheets and come back.

  • Every error has a next step

    Say what happened, then the way out.

The design

Twelve screens, from number to paid.

Scroll sideways. Tap any screen to see it larger.

Six decisions that shaped it

Decision 1

Identify with a mobile number, not an account.

Forced sign-up made 18% of abandoners leave.1 Indian shoppers already know the mobile number and OTP check from banking and delivery apps. One field and one button leave little to get wrong.

Trade-off: an OTP on each new site or device.

Decision 2

Make the one step they cannot do alone forgiving.

The number shows with a Change link, the resend timer is visible, and the error sits beside Resend. Expired codes, too many attempts and alternate verification each have a state.

Trade-off: six boxes look clear but need careful autoread and paste in build.

Decision 3

Show what an address will cost, and when it arrives.

Extra costs (40%) and slow delivery (20%) are top reasons to leave.1 The saved address carries a delivery promise, and four options show date and price before payment.

Trade-off: one more decision, so the option that matches the promise is pre-selected.

Decision 4

Keep the new address short, and help fill it.

Seven fields, against an average of 11.3.2 PIN code comes first so city and state follow it,3 and typing a house number suggests saved addresses.

Trade-off: auto-filled city and state can be wrong, so they stay editable.

Decision 5

Keep offers in view, but off the main path.

Shoppers who hunt for a code often leave to find one. An offer strip shows the count, one sheet holds them all, and an applied offer stays visible on every step.

Trade-off: coupons and payment offers share a sheet but behave differently.

Decision 6

Preferred ways to pay first, and a way back from failure.

Saved cards sit at the top with their cashback, UPI apps are one tap away, and the button states the amount. A failed payment gives the reason in plain words and other ways to pay.

Trade-off: cash on delivery as a retry saves the order but raises return risk.

What I would improve

Two things I would change next.

Medium

Skip the OTP for a known device

Returning shoppers still verify on every new site. Offer “remember this device”, with consent, so a recognised shopper can skip the code.4

Watch

Cash on delivery as a retry

It rescues orders when UPI fails, but COD orders are returned far more often than prepaid ones.7 Make it a merchant setting, and track returns by how the order was recovered.

Measuring it

How I would know it works.

  • Checkout completionDo more people who tap Place order finish paying?
  • Payment successDo payments go through once attempted?
  • Time to payHow fast is it, for each kind of shopper?
  • OTP success and resendsIs verification a hurdle?
  • Address-related delivery failuresDoes address help reduce bad deliveries?
  • Retry to successDoes the failure sheet recover orders?

Usability tests

Five to eight people per segment, on real phones.

  1. Pay as a returning shopper with a saved address and card.
  2. Pay as a first-time shopper, adding an address.
  3. Recover from a wrong OTP.
  4. Recover from a failed payment.
  5. Find and apply an offer.
