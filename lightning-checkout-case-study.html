Case study: Paytm Payment Gateway

A checkout that knows you by your phone number

Lightning Checkout lets a shopper pay on a merchant’s website with a mobile number, a one-time code and a saved address. I led its design from the first screen to the failure states.

Product
Lightning Checkout, part of Paytm Payment Gateway
Platform
Mobile web. Opens when a shopper taps Place order on a merchant site
Scope
Screens and states, from sign-in to payment failure
Status
Designed and built. First merchant integration pending

In short

The problem

Shoppers retype the same name, address and payment details on every site. Baymard’s survey data shows 17% of shoppers who abandon a cart blame a long or complicated checkout.1

The solution

Identify the shopper by mobile number, verify with a one-time code, then reuse their saved addresses and preferred ways to pay.

My role

I owned the flow architecture, interaction design, visual design and the states for when things go wrong.

Design brief

Merchants who sell online need a checkout they can add to their storefront without building one. Lightning Checkout adds an accelerated flow on top of Paytm Payment Gateway. It recognises a returning shopper, brings their saved addresses and preferred payment methods, and keeps offers in view.

Objective
Design an end-to-end mobile checkout that is faster for returning shoppers, clear for first-time shoppers, and recoverable when something fails.
Users
  • Shoppers on a phone, both returning and first-time
  • Merchants who add the checkout to their website through their storefront platform
Constraints
  • Opens over a merchant’s page, so it must feel trustworthy under someone else’s brand
  • Indian payment mix: UPI, cards, net banking, EMI and cash on delivery
  • Must handle OTP failures, undeliverable PIN codes and UPI network downtime
  • Must work for first-time shoppers who have no saved data
Success measures
Checkout completion rate, payment success rate, time from Place order to payment, OTP success rate, and address-related delivery failures.

Problem statement

Online shoppers re-enter the same contact, address and payment details on every merchant site. That slows them down, invites address mistakes and gives them more places to quit. Merchants have no ready-made checkout that remembers a shopper they have never met.

How might we…

  • let a returning shopper pay without retyping what we already know?
  • keep a first-time shopper’s address entry short and hard to get wrong?
  • keep price, offers and trust in view so nobody is surprised at the last step?
  • make every failure end with a next step instead of a dead end?

What I took from it

  • Accounts cost sales

    18% of abandoners left because a site asked them to create an account. I identify shoppers by mobile number and OTP, with no password to invent or remember.

  • Fields cost sales

    The average checkout asks for 11.3 fields. A returning shopper in this flow types two inputs, a mobile number and a code. A first-time shopper fills seven address fields, inside Baymard’s 8-field benchmark.

  • Surprises cost sales

    Extra costs are the top reason to leave, at 40%. Delivery date and charge appear on the address step, and the order total stays on screen from start to finish.

  • Failures need an exit

    Card declines and site errors each cost sales. Every failure state names the problem and offers a way forward, usually another payment method.

  • India’s payment mix is its own

    UPI runs at national scale, and most orders are still cash on delivery. UPI apps get first-class treatment in the payment step. COD is an option with a visible fee, because returns on COD orders cost merchants far more than on prepaid ones.

Design principles

  • Know the shopper, don’t interrogate

    Ask for what we cannot know. Fill in the rest.

  • Keep the price in view

    The total, the offers and the delivery cost never hide.

  • One decision per screen

    Side tasks open in sheets and return to the same place.

  • Every error has a next step

    Say what happened, then offer the way out.

The design

The flow in twelve screens

A selection of key screens from the flow. Scroll sideways.

Six decisions

1. Identify with a mobile number, not an account

Why. Forced account creation made 18% of abandoners leave.1 A mobile number and OTP is a check Indian shoppers already know from banking and delivery apps. One field and one button leaves little to get wrong.

Trade-off. A shopper meets an OTP on each new site or device. Review finding 1 looks at skipping it.

2. Make the OTP step forgiving

Why. The OTP is the one step the shopper cannot do alone. The number is shown with a Change link, the resend timer is visible, and the error appears next to Resend OTP. I also designed states for an expired code, too many attempts and alternate verification.

Trade-off. Six separate boxes look clear but need careful autoread and paste handling in build.

3. Show what an address choice will cost and when it arrives

Why. Extra costs (40%) and slow delivery (20%) are two of the top reasons to leave.1 The saved address carries a delivery promise, and the four delivery options show date and price before payment. An address that cannot be served is tagged in the list.

Trade-off. Delivery options add a decision to the address step. I pre-select the option that matches the promise.

4. Keep the new-address form short, and help fill it

Why. The average checkout has 11.3 fields and most sites need 8.2 This form has seven, with PIN code first so city and state can follow it.3 Typing a house number suggests saved addresses, and Save as lets a shopper label the result.

Trade-off. Auto-filled city and state can go unnoticed or be wrong, so they stay editable.

5. Keep offers in view, but off the main path

Why. Shoppers who hunt for a code often leave to find one. An offer strip shows the count, one sheet holds everything, and an applied offer stays visible on every step. The promo field sits inside the sheet, which matches Baymard’s advice to hide it.2

Trade-off. Coupons and payment offers share a sheet but behave differently.

6. Put preferred ways to pay first, and plan for failure

Why. Saved cards sit at the top with their cashback, UPI apps are one tap away, and the Pay button states the amount. When a payment fails, the sheet gives the reason in plain words and offers other methods. Card declines (10%) and thin method choice (9%) both cost sales.1

Trade-off. Offering cash on delivery as a retry saves the order but raises delivery risk. See review finding 2.

Designing for the unhappy path

More than a quarter of the screens are error, recovery or edge states. Each one says what happened and what to do next.

Identity

  • Incorrect number
  • Incorrect OTP
  • OTP expired
  • Attempts exceeded
  • Alternate verification
  • OTP sent again

Address and delivery

  • Wrong PIN code
  • Mandatory field missing
  • Checking service availability
  • Address not serviceable
  • Delivery unavailable
  • No address found
  • No email on file

Payment

  • Incorrect card number
  • Card OTP
  • Payment failed, retry sheet
  • UPI network down, collapsed
  • UPI network down, expanded

System

  • API or technical failure
  • Merchant error
  • Generic error
  • Back button pressed mid-flow

What I would improve

  1. Medium

    1. An OTP on every new site or device

    Returning shoppers still verify each time. Offer “remember this device” with consent, so a recognised shopper can skip the code. Shopify says Shop Pay recognises many returning shoppers without any action, though that is its own claim.4

  2. Watch

    2. Cash on delivery as a retry option

    It rescues orders when UPI fails, but Razorpay reports 25–30% of COD orders are returned against 2–3% for prepaid, though it gives no source.7 Make COD and its fee a merchant setting, and track returns by the way the order was recovered.

Measuring it

Lightning Checkout is not live yet, so there are no outcomes to report. Here is how I would find out whether it works. Targets would be set from each merchant’s current checkout, not guessed in advance.

Usability tests

Moderated sessions with five to eight participants per segment, on real phones. Tasks:

  • Pay as a returning shopper with a saved address and a saved card.
  • Pay as a first-time shopper, adding a new address.
  • Recover from a wrong OTP.
  • Recover from a failed payment.
  • Find and apply an offer.

I would record whether each task is finished without help, time on task, and where people hesitate or misread a screen.
